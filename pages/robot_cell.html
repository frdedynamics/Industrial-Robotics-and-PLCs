Robot cell

Project objective

In this part of the project, you will build and program a simulated robot cell in Visual Components (VC) and then coordinate it with a PLC program running in CODESYS Control Win SL. The cell transports a raw part to a robot, loads it into a CNC mill, waits for machining, unloads it, and returns the finished part to a conveyor.

This page guides you through the work in the intended order, from assembling and testing the VC-only cell to connecting and commissioning the PLC-controlled version. Use it as a working tutorial: later steps build on the checks completed in the earlier steps.

The work is divided into two stages:

  1. Build and program a complete working cell using internal VC signals.
  2. Transfer overall cell coordination to a PLC connected through OPC UA.

Do not begin with the PLC connection. First establish that the robot poses, motions, gripper, CNC model, and complete material flow work inside VC. This gives you a known-working plant before communication and distributed control are introduced.

By completing the project, you should be able to:

  • configure a robot, gripper, conveyor, sensor, and process machine in VC,
  • divide a robot task into meaningful reusable subroutines,
  • define a clear control boundary between a PLC and robot controller,
  • design two controllers that coordinate through a command handshake,
  • connect CODESYS and VC using OPC UA, and
  • commission the system incrementally rather than debugging everything at once.

Required cell behavior

The completed system shall repeat the following material flow:

  1. Transport a raw part to the robot pickup position.
  2. Stop the part and conveyor at the pickup position.
  3. Pick the part and load it into the CNC mill.
  4. Close the CNC door and start machining.
  5. Confirm that machining starts and wait until it finishes.
  6. Open the door and unload the finished part.
  7. Place the part on the outgoing conveyor.
  8. Return the robot to its home pose and prepare for the next part.

The first version is controlled by the robot program inside VC. In the final version, the PLC controls the overall sequence while the robot executes requested motion operations.

Build the simulated cell

Organize the project with sublayouts

Build the robot cell in the same VC file as the completed conveyor system, but do not connect the two systems yet. Begin from the conveyor checkpoint and keep its saved copy unchanged as a tested backup. Place the new robot cell some distance away from the conveyor system, with enough empty space that the two material flows remain clearly separate during development.

The conveyor-system instructions create a Conveyor system sublayout. Confirm that the completed conveyor components are assigned to it, then create a second sublayout named Robot cell. Components for the new cell are moved into this second sublayout after their initial placement.

The roles of the Cell Graph, components, and sublayouts are introduced in Layouts and components. This structure keeps the complete system in one simulation while making each section easier to select, move, copy, and inspect. The linked section also contains the relevant VC Academy lesson. Sublayout visibility is an organizational aid; hiding a sublayout does not replace disconnecting or stopping components when you want to test only one part of the system.

Design the robot cell

Start with the Simple Robot Pedestal and the KUKA robot. Place the pedestal in the 3D world and position the robot on top; you can use the attach tool to be able to move them together. The height of the pedestal can be adjusted as needed, and it is part of the cell design: it determines whether the robot can approach the conveyor and CNC at useful joint configurations, not only whether the TCP can reach them at all.

Next, add a short incoming conveyor and place a Conveyor Sensor close to its downstream end. This sensor defines the repeatable pickup location. Copy and paste the Advanced Feeder that is already configured in the conveyor system, and configure only this copy to generate grey cylinders. Keep the original mixed feeder unchanged in the Conveyor system sublayout. The temporary feeder provides test parts immediately while the robot cell is developed on its own.

Place the CNC mill and outgoing conveyor provisionally. Their first positions do not need to be final. Leave enough room to open and access the CNC door, and provide clearance for the gripper, workpiece, and approach and retract motions.

The final cell layout is your design decision, and there is no prescribed arrangement. Among the choices are the relative positions and orientations of the robot, conveyors, CNC mill, and access areas. For example, the conveyors could be arranged in line or at an angle, and the CNC mill could be placed beside the robot or across from the conveyors. These are only starting points; you may develop another arrangement that gives a good combination of robot reach, clear motion paths, material flow, and access to the equipment.

Check reachability and clearance

The VC reachability overview explains how workspace profiles, jogging, joint checks, and collision detection complement one another.

Use the robot’s WorkSpace2D or WorkSpace3D component properties to display its approximate workspace, also called its work envelope. Use this envelope as rough guidance for the initial cell layout and for estimating basic distances between the robot, conveyors, and CNC mill. Figure 1 shows the WorkSpace2D profile around the Agilus robot used in this project. The linked VC overview contains the relevant Academy lessons.

This profile approximates position reach; it does not prove that the robot can achieve the required tool orientation at every point. The distinction between reachable and dexterous workspace explains why checking the complete task poses is necessary.

For more detailed and reliable tuning of the arrangement, mount the gripper (see Section 4.3 for how to mount and use it), generate one grey cylinder, and grip it. Then use the Jog tool to move the complete robot, tool, and workpiece assembly through the important task poses. After all, it is the workpiece that must be placed at the positions and orientations required by the task. The cell must therefore allow the robot to move the workpiece through these poses without exceeding joint limits, approaching singularities, or colliding with the surrounding equipment. While jogging, use the Jog panel to monitor each joint’s current value and permitted range. This shows when a target pose pushes one or more joints toward their limits. Enable Color Highlight in the Limits group to also mark the affected robot geometry in red when a joint limit is exceeded. Enable a collision detector and start the simulation once to initialize it. You can then reset the simulation and use the detector while jogging; collisions with the CNC, conveyors, pedestal, and other equipment are highlighted in yellow in the 3D view.

Manual jogging checks individual poses but does not validate every point along the programmed motion between them. After teaching representative motions, run the program with collision detection and the Limits tools enabled, including the singularity check.

Before teaching the complete program, verify that:

  • the robot can reach the pickup, CNC loading, outgoing conveyor, and home poses,
  • the gripper and workpiece fit through the CNC opening,
  • the robot can approach and retract without crossing the machine or fence,
  • the conveyor sensor is located at a repeatable pickup pose.

These checks are easier to correct before many robot positions depend on the layout.

Consider safety while the layout develops

Do not wait until the end of the project to think about safety, but also do not spend time drawing final fencing while the equipment is still moving between tests. During layout design, consider where people need access for loading, teaching, clearing faults, and maintenance; where parts and people could enter the cell intentionally or unintentionally; where a gate could be placed; and whether robot motion creates trapping points against the CNC mill, fence, or other fixed equipment.

Once the equipment positions are mostly stable and the relevant robot motions have been checked, use the Fence and Wall Builder from Facilities - Factory to draw the perimeter caging. This is usually the most convenient way to create and revise the fence. Simulation does not replace a risk assessment, but it makes alternative layouts easier to examine before positions and control logic become difficult to change. The safety process and the priority given to reducing hazards through design are discussed in the risk-assessment process and the risk-reduction priorities on the robot safety page.

After placing the cell equipment, select the newly added components and use Move to Layout to assign them to the Robot cell sublayout. Check the Layouts tab afterwards to make sure that no cell component remains at the ROOT level. Moving the components together is usually quicker than assigning each component immediately after adding it.

Mount and configure the gripper

Mount the Generic 3 Finger Gripper directly on the robot flange using PnP. Move the gripper toward the flange until the compatible physical interfaces are highlighted, then continue moving it until the components snap together. This physical connection makes the gripper follow the flange when the robot is jogged. The Add a robot tool chapter in the Introduction to Robot Teaching video demonstrates this workflow.

Enable automatic I/O connection when mounting the gripper. This option is enabled by default and avoids having to connect the gripper’s input and output signals to the robot manually.

Before configuring these signals, it is important to understand how standard grasping works in VC. A grasp action searches for products inside a detection volume around a selected tool frame. When a product is found, VC fixes its pose relative to that frame, so the product moves with the robot. A release action removes this logical attachment. Physical finger contact is not calculated, which is why VC can grasp a product even when no gripper model is mounted or the fingers do not touch it. VC also provides rigid-body physics for custom contact-based simulations, but that is a separate, more complex approach and is not used in this project.

The finger animation is therefore not what holds the product in the standard simulation, but it is still important. Realistic open and closed widths let you check the gripper’s geometric capacity and its clearance around the workpiece. They also make collision checking more meaningful because an open gripper occupies a different volume from a closed one. Visible finger motion helps you understand and debug the program sequence, and it makes the simulation clearer and more convincing in customer or sales presentations. It does not validate real gripping force, payload capacity, deformation, or slip.

The general relationship between robot I/O ports, component signals, and Actions Configuration is introduced in Robot I/O and signal actions. The configuration below applies those mechanisms specifically to the project gripper.

The logical grasp and release actions are configured in the robot’s Actions Configuration properties. In most VC robot components, outputs 1 to 16 are predefined for grasping and releasing with the default tool frames. These outputs can be used for a tool without movable joints, such as a suction cup, or even without a visible gripper. In that case, the output does not need to be connected to another component: setting it only triggers the robot’s internal grasp or release action. Always inspect the selected robot’s mappings because component models can differ.

A gripper with movable fingers requires both parts of the simulation. The robot output must trigger VC’s internal grasp or release action and drive the gripper joint so that the fingers close or open. After mounting the gripper, inspect its Control properties and the robot’s signal connections. Automatically connected custom gripper I/O commonly starts at robot port 100.

In the tested cell, robot output 100 is connected to the gripper input IN_J1_Action: TRUE closes the fingers and FALSE opens them. Configure the same output in Actions Configuration so that TRUE also performs Grasp and FALSE performs Release. Select a tool frame at the centre of the gripping region and set a detection volume that contains the cylinder at the grasp pose without including nearby products. It is usually easiest to align one of the robot’s native tool frames with the gripper’s TCP after mounting the gripper, and then use that frame for both the grasp action and the robot motion statements. Figure 2 shows the signal connection and action configuration together. Section 4.4 explains the TCP setup in more detail.

Opening and closing the gripper takes time. After commanding it, read its separate open-state or closed-state feedback and wait until the requested state has been reached before moving the robot. The Test the signals chapter in the Model a robot gripper, part II lesson demonstrates this I/O and feedback workflow.

Finally, use VC’s measure tool to determine the cylinder size and set the gripper’s J1_OpenValue and J1_CloseValue properties accordingly. Run the sequence and confirm that the fingers fully clear the cylinder when open and close to a realistic gripping width.

Define and select the TCP

The attached gripper contributes a TCP frame. Select a frame located at the centre of the gripping region and use it as the active tool frame in the robot motion statements. Inspect its position and orientation rather than assuming that the component’s default frame is suitable. If necessary, move one of the robot’s native tool frames to the required TCP and use that frame consistently throughout the program.

The frame should be located and oriented according to the physical task, not only at a convenient point in the 3D model. Review frames and poses if the TCP or reference-frame behavior is unclear.

CNC mill

Use the Process Machine - CNC component. Configure a short process time during development and inspect its command and feedback signals:

CNC signal Direction Meaning
FROM_PLC_OpenDoor Into CNC Request the door to open.
FROM_PLC_CloseDoor Into CNC Request the door to close.
FROM_PLC_StartProcess Into CNC Start the machining process.
TO_PLC_DoorsOpen From CNC Door-open feedback.
TO_PLC_DoorsClosed From CNC Door-closed feedback.
TO_PLC_ProcessIsRunning From CNC Machining is currently running.

There is no separate process-complete signal in this model. The controller must first observe ProcessIsRunning = TRUE and then wait for it to return to FALSE. Waiting only for FALSE could incorrectly report completion before the process has started.

Stage 1: internal VC control

The general workflows for creating robot motions and organizing a VC robot program are introduced in Jogging and teaching motion and Program structure and logic. Apply them here to build the project-specific sequence.

Connect the pickup sensor and CNC signals to available robot input and output ports. The robot program is now responsible for the complete cell: it waits for a part, operates the CNC, and performs all robot and gripper motions.

During this stage, the detected part must remain at the taught pickup pose. The simplest option is to configure the Conveyor Sensor to StopPart when it detects a product. Alternatively, route the sensor signal so that detection sets the incoming conveyor’s PowerOn signal to FALSE. This alternative requires an inverted signal or equivalent routing logic; connecting a sensor whose active value is TRUE directly to PowerOn would have the opposite effect. In either case, verify that the part stops before teaching the pickup pose and that the conveyor does not restart while the robot is gripping it.

Use meaningful subroutines

Do not write the complete task as one long Main routine. At minimum, organize the motions into operations corresponding to:

  • picking a part from the conveyor,
  • moving to and loading the CNC,
  • running the CNC process,
  • unloading the CNC and placing the finished part, and
  • returning home.

Choose names that describe what each operation achieves. Each routine should start and finish in a predictable situation and contain the details tightly coupled to that operation. For example, the pick routine should operate the gripper and verify its feedback before retracting.

The internal Main routine should read like the material flow rather than a list of individual robot motions. This makes the program easier to test now and provides subroutines that can be requested by the PLC later.

Validate before continuing

Run repeated complete cycles and verify:

  • all robot positions and configurations are reachable,
  • approach and retract paths do not collide,
  • the correct base and tool frames are active,
  • the gripper reaches its commanded state before the next motion,
  • the part is attached and released at the expected points,
  • the CNC door and process sequence completes correctly, and
  • the robot ends each cycle at a known home pose.

Do not proceed to external PLC control until this version works reliably.

Moving equipment after teaching positions

If you must relocate or rotate the robot after teaching positions, change Lock Positions from To reference to To World before moving it. Change the setting back afterwards. This holds the taught poses fixed in the world while the robot base moves.

For equipment-relative tasks, consider defining robot base frames on the relevant equipment instead. A pose defined relative to a CNC fixture then moves with that fixture, which can make later layout changes easier. Choose the method that reflects which physical relationship should remain fixed.

Integrate the conveyor and robot-cell sublayouts

After the internally controlled robot program works reliably with the temporary feeder, connect the two subsystems. Disconnect or remove the robot cell’s feeder, move the Conveyor system and Robot cell sublayouts together, and connect the appropriate conveyor paths. Start the original mixed feeder in the Conveyor system sublayout only after this connection is working.

Run the complete material flow again before adding PLC control. Check not only whether the robot cycle still runs, but also whether parts queue at the expected locations, whether upstream conveyors become blocked, and whether the timing between part arrival, conveyor stopping, and robot pickup remains reliable.

Stage 2: external PLC control

The distinction between VC-internal signals and values mapped to an external PLC is introduced in Internal VC control and external PLC control. The following sections define the project-specific control boundary and interface.

The final system separates cell coordination from robot motion. Sensors and machine actuators are mapped to CODESYS, while the PLC and robot exchange high-level operation commands and status values.

Controller Responsibility
PLC Run and stop control for the ordinary conveyors, pickup-sensor evaluation, CNC door and process control, and overall operation order. Product routing and the complete pusher transfer remain internal to VC; timeout and fault handling are optional extensions.
Robot program Robot motion, approach and retract paths, gripper actions, and gripper feedback within each requested operation.

The gripper remains under robot control because its actions are tightly coupled to the pick and place motions. The PLC sequence controls the ordinary conveyor run command and CNC equipment, while the Pusher Conveyor retains its internal routing and transfer behavior.

Remove the direct conveyor-sensor and CNC connections from the active robot program. Retain the local robot-to-gripper connections. Keep the internally controlled routine as a tested reference while developing a separate PLC-controlled main routine.

Two interacting state machines

A useful way to structure the control is to use a state machine on each side, but at different levels:

  • The PLC FSM represents progress through the complete material flow and decides which device may act next.
  • The robot-side FSM accepts one operation request, executes the selected robot subroutine, and reports its status.

The PLC must not calculate robot joint motion, and the robot must not silently continue the complete production sequence after one requested operation. Both controllers should be understandable and testable independently.

Robot command properties

On the VC Modeling tab, add three integer properties to the robot component. Place them in a group named PLC:

Property Direction Purpose
PLC::Command PLC to robot Requested operation; zero means no command.
PLC::RobotState Robot to PLC State of the robot command interface.
PLC::ActiveCommand Robot to PLC Command accepted and currently being handled.

PLC is only the property-group name. In a robot-program expression, the :: syntax separates the group and property names.

Use the following interface contract:

Command Required robot operation
0 No command or PLC acknowledgement
1 Pick the part from the conveyor
2 Load the CNC
3 Unload the CNC and place the part on the conveyor
4 Move to the home pose
Robot state Meaning
0 Initializing
1 Ready for a command
2 Busy executing the accepted command
3 Command completed and waiting for acknowledgement
4 Invalid command or fault

The robot program must use PLC::ActiveCommand to choose the required subroutine. You can implement this selection with If and Else statements or with a Switch Case. Switch Case is often compact and easy to read when each integer command corresponds to one operation.

Required command handshake

For every robot operation:

  1. The PLC waits for robot state Ready.
  2. The PLC writes and holds the requested command.
  3. The robot copies it to ActiveCommand and reports Busy.
  4. The PLC verifies that the intended command was accepted.
  5. The robot executes the complete subroutine and reports Done.
  6. The PLC clears Command to 0 as acknowledgement.
  7. The robot clears ActiveCommand and returns to Ready.

This level-based handshake is preferable to short pulses because a pulse could be missed between an OPC UA update and a PLC scan.

Tip

VC robot programs provide a dedicated wait statement only for binary robot inputs. To wait for an integer component property, use a While loop with a short delay, for example 0.05 s. The loop condition should describe when the program must continue waiting. The delay prevents an empty polling loop from consuming simulation execution time.

OPC UA interface

The PLC-integration and virtual-commissioning section explains the complete signal path and provides the relevant VC Academy setup lesson.

Expose the required CODESYS variables through Symbol Configuration and connect VC to the CODESYS OPC UA server. With CODESYS Control Win SL on the same computer, the server address is normally:

opc.tcp://localhost:4840

VC acts as the OPC UA client. Use separate Connectivity variable groups for values sent in each direction.

PLC variable VC property or signal Direction
iRobotCommand PLC::Command PLC to VC
xConveyorRun Shared PowerOnSignal for the ordinary conveyors PLC to VC
xCncOpenDoor FROM_PLC_OpenDoor PLC to VC
xCncCloseDoor FROM_PLC_CloseDoor PLC to VC
xCncStartProcess FROM_PLC_StartProcess PLC to VC
PLC::RobotState iRobotState VC to PLC
PLC::ActiveCommand iRobotActiveCommand VC to PLC
Pickup sensor xPartAtPick VC to PLC
TO_PLC_DoorsOpen xCncDoorOpen VC to PLC
TO_PLC_DoorsClosed xCncDoorClosed VC to PLC
TO_PLC_ProcessIsRunning xCncProcessRunning VC to PLC

If you retained the sensor’s internal StopPart action from Stage 1, it can continue to hold the product at the exact taught pose despite communication delay. The PLC must still turn off the conveyor when it receives xPartAtPick, preventing the conveyor and following products from continuing. Document the internal stop as a simulation convenience rather than PLC behavior.

Commissioning procedure

Test the connected system incrementally:

  1. Start CODESYS Control Win SL and run the PLC application with production disabled and iRobotCommand = 0.
  2. Connect both OPC UA variable groups in VC.
  3. Reset and start the VC simulation.
  4. Confirm that the robot initializes, moves home, and reports Ready.
  5. Test each robot command and its complete handshake individually.
  6. Test the conveyor sensor and each CNC command and feedback pair.
  7. Enable production and run the complete PLC-controlled cycle.

Monitor the PLC FSM state, robot command, robot state, active command, pickup sensor, conveyor output, CNC door feedback, and process-running feedback. When the system stops, first identify which controller and state is waiting, then check the condition required to leave that state.

Requirements and deliverables

Your submission shall include:

  • the VC layout with functional geometry, configured tool and signals,
  • the internally controlled robot program used for initial validation,
  • the PLC-controlled robot program and meaningful motion subroutines,
  • the CODESYS PLC program,
  • an I/O list showing every OPC UA variable and its direction,
  • diagrams or tables documenting the PLC and robot state machines, and
  • evidence that the internal and externally controlled cycles were tested.

The externally controlled solution shall satisfy these acceptance criteria:

  • the PLC, not the robot, coordinates ordinary conveyor motion and the CNC,
  • the Pusher Conveyor retains its tested internal routing and transfer behavior,
  • the robot accepts only defined operation commands,
  • every command follows the specified acknowledgement handshake,
  • robot commands are held until acknowledged rather than sent as short pulses,
  • the CNC process is confirmed to start before completion is evaluated,
  • disabling production prevents another cycle without interrupting an active robot or CNC operation,
  • the robot and gripper finish each requested operation in a predictable state, and
  • the complete material flow repeats without manual intervention.

Develop the normal sequence first. Timeouts and fault recovery are optional extensions and should be added only after the nominal cycle and communication interface are stable.
